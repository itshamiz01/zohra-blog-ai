# PROMPT 1 — STORY DNA + NOVEL BIBLE ENGINE
## *The Secret He Married*

---

## §1. PREMISE (one paragraph, the spine everything hangs from)

Twelve years ago the coastal freighter **Belladonna** ran aground on Gannet Rock in fog and
went to pieces with forty-one men aboard. The town of Craye Harbor decided the master,
**Tobias Vane**, was drunk and incompetent, and the maritime assurance house that had
underwritten her, **Crane & Voss**, paid the claim and was quietly saved from ruin.

Tobias Vane's daughter was fifteen years old and aboard that ship, hidden in the chain
locker by her father. She is the only living person who saw what happened below decks.
She has spent twelve years under a false name, believing she is being hunted, and she is right.

Now **Elliot Crane** — sole heir of Crane & Voss, the man whose signature certified the
*Belladonna* seaworthy — has found her. He does not want her dead. He wants her *married*.

So does she, for her own reasons. Neither of them tells the other the truth about why.

## §2. GENRE CALIBRATION

| Axis | Setting |
|---|---|
| Primary | Dark romance |
| Secondary | Psychological thriller / maritime legal thriller |
| Moral gradient | **Medium-dark with Extreme-dark episodes** — coercion, confinement, one on-page killing, sexual tension built on unequal power but explicitly negotiated at the critical junctures |
| Reader contract | We promise danger and deliver danger. We promise genuine psychological cost and we pay it. We never promise safety we don't intend to give. |
| Heat | 18+. Five full intimate sequences, none gratuitous, every one a power negotiation that changes the board |
| Ending register | Earned, partial, unsettled. The couple survives. The question does not resolve. |
| Register | Cold water, warm rooms. Salt, paper, iron, wool, wet stone. |

**The promise of the book, in one line:** *A marriage built as a legal instrument, and the
two people who ruin themselves discovering it was also a marriage.*

---

## §3. THEMES

1. **Paper and water.** What survives, what dissolves. She is a conservator: she repairs
   documents for a living. Her whole profession is *the fight against water.* The men who
   drowned were killed to destroy paper. Everything in this novel is a document fighting
   the sea.
2. **The arithmetic of other people's lives.** Cordelia Voss is not a sadist. She is an
   actuary. The novel's central horror is that forty-one lives were *priced*, correctly, by
   an intelligent woman, and the price was acceptable.
3. **Legibility as a cage.** Marriage, wills, probate, spousal privilege — every legal
   structure that is supposed to protect Wren is a structure that renders her silent property.
   The law is the villain's best tool and the heroine's only weapon.
4. **Testimony.** Who is allowed to speak, and in what room. The novel ends in a courtroom
   because that is the only place in the world where a woman who was fifteen is finally
   permitted to be a witness instead of a wife.
5. **The unreliability of the survivor's memory.** Wren's narration is honest and wrong.

## §5. WORLD RULES (hard, non-negotiable, ledger-enforced)

**The Maritime Rule.** The wreck of the *Belladonna* lies in territorial water on Gannet Rock,
in the tidal race four miles off Marrow Point. She is visible at extreme low water — a black
ribcage above the swell. She is legally *in the custody of the Admiralty Marshal* and her
recovered effects are held in probate. Nothing may be lifted without the underwriter's consent.

**The Vane Instrument.** Tobias Vane's will contains a clause drafted by Crane & Voss's own
solicitors:
> *"All that remains of my command, and all papers recovered from her, and all rights of
> salvage attaching thereto, I leave to my daughter Wren upon her lawful marriage, to be
> held jointly with her husband as her lord and protector; and until such marriage, to be
> held in trust and unopened."*

Consequence: an unmarried Wren cannot access her father's effects. A married Wren's rights
flow to her husband. **This is the trap. It was designed by the villain. It is the reason
both protagonists walk into it.**

**Spousal Privilege.** A wife cannot be compelled to testify against her husband in a criminal
matter. Elliot knows this. Wren does not — until Chapter 28.

**The Reverter.** A schedule attached to the Crane & Voss partnership deed: if Elliot Crane's
marriage is dissolved, void, or childless at the expiration of one year, the Vane salvage
rights revert to the partnership. Cordelia wrote it. Elliot has never read it.

**The Seal.** The letter at Tarrant & Quill may be opened only (a) on Wren's death or
(b) with Wren's express consent in the presence of the court. Cordelia has wanted it opened
for eight years and cannot force it. This is why Wren is still alive.

**Physical Law.** Fog is a character. The tide is a clock. The causeway to Marrow Point floods
twice a day and is impassable for four hours at springs. **When the causeway is under water,
there are exactly two ways off the point: the boat, and the rocks.** Every act of violence
in this novel is timed to the tide.

## §6. SETTING AS PSYCHOLOGY

**MARROW POINT.** A black granite headland, four miles out, with a decommissioned signal
station on its crown and a house built into the lee of it in 1881 for the keeper's family.
Nine windows. All of them face the water. Elliot Crane bought it eleven years ago and has
lived in it alone. He lives directly above the thing he buried, and he watches it through
binoculars at low tide like a man checking a wound.

*What it means:* a house with no interior — every room looks outward. Two people who cannot
stop watching the sea, and therefore cannot stop watching each other.

**THE LOCKED ROOM.** The northwest room. Forty-one brass plates on the wall, one for each
dead man, polished weekly by Elliot himself. And one blank plate. The forty-second.

**CRAYE HARBOR.** A dying deepwater port with good bones: granite insurance houses, a
customs hall with a leaking roof, a chandlery that now sells coffee. Crane & Voss occupies
the fourth floor of the Assurance Building. Cordelia Voss has never moved her office from
the room where the claim was signed.

**THE BINDERY.** Wren's workshop. Dry, warm, smelling of wheat paste and leather and
methyl cellulose. The one room in the novel with no windows on the sea. It is the only place
she feels safe, and it is the first thing she loses.

## §7. FOUR-PART ARCHITECTURE

### PART I — THE OFFER (Ch 1–9)
**The brain of this part:** *Acquisition.* Everything is a transaction dressed as a kindness.
The reader must feel the pleasure of a beautiful cage being built around someone who can
see it being built and climbs in anyway.
*Ends with:* Wren reading the instrument. She learns she is a deed. She stays.
*Reader feeling:* delicious dread.

### PART II — THE HOUSE (Ch 10–19)
**The brain of this part:** *Duel.* Two liars in one house. The romance is conducted as
warfare — every kindness is a feint. Then the salvage claim gives both of them a deadline,
and desire gets tangled into the strategy until neither can tell which move was the seduction.
*Ends with:* Elliot choosing silence and letting her be arrested. The house wins. He doesn't.
*Reader feeling:* betrayal, and complicity in it.

### PART III — THE WRECK (Ch 20–29)
**The brain of this part:** *Descent.* The enemy becomes the only honest voice. Wren goes
down into the truth layer by layer — the rescue boat, the dock at 4 a.m., the reverter,
and finally her own memory and her own handwriting. Every chapter takes something from her.
*Ends with:* divorce papers. "Sign it. Then say everything."
*Reader feeling:* grief, vertigo, the floor going out.

### PART IV — WHAT WE SAVE (Ch 30–37)
**The brain of this part:** *Reckoning and price.* No more secrets, only consequences.
The grovel is not a speech; it is the systematic surrender of everything Elliot Crane is.
The truth is spoken in a courtroom by a woman who is no longer anyone's wife.
*Ends with:* the year expiring. The question alive.
*Reader feeling:* the specific ache of a happy ending that cost too much.

## §9. WHAT MAKES THIS DIFFERENT FROM THE OTHER NINETY-NINE

Most dark romance makes the male lead dangerous and the heroine his captive.
This one makes them **co-conspirators in the same fraud from page one**, each convinced the
other is the mark. The power is genuinely symmetrical and genuinely unequal at the same time,
because the *law* is on his side and the *truth* is on hers — and neither can spend what
they hold without destroying the other.

The other difference: **the heroine's unreliability is the twist.** She is not lying to the
reader to protect herself. She is lying to herself about a document she invented at nineteen
because a girl with no family needed to be the keeper of something. When she finds out, the
reader has to reread 300 pages of her narration and find the seams. The clues are all there,
hidden in characterization: she can describe the log's binding but never its contents; she
remembers the smell of the ink but not one sentence; she has never once quoted it.

---

## §10. CONTENT BOUNDARIES

On the page: coercive marriage, confinement by tide and by contract, one strangulation,
one staged drowning, a fourteen-year-old's memory of forty-one men dying, panicked
hyperventilation, dissociation, gaslighting by an institution, and five intimate sequences
that are explicit in *psychology* and sensual in *detail* while stopping short of
graphic pornography — because per Skill 3, every intimate scene must deepen character
psychology, and the moment it becomes anatomy it stops being this book.

Not on the page: sexual violence. The darkness in this novel is contractual, not carnal.
