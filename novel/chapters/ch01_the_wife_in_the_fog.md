# CHAPTER ONE
## THE WIFE IN THE FOG

*Wren*

---

### I

Four hours into my marriage, I understood two things: that my husband intended to keep me, and that he had never once been honest with me about why.

The second was not a surprise. The first was.

---

The registry office in Craye Harbor is on the second floor of a building that used to be a customs house, and the stairs still have the brass nosing worn down in the middle by two hundred years of men carrying other people's cargo up them. There were three witnesses. One was a woman from the registrar's office who kept her coat on. One was my husband's solicitor, whose name I had known for eleven months and who had never once looked at my face. The third was a man I did not know at all, standing at the back with his hat in his hands, and it was only as I was signing that I understood he had been brought in off the street and paid, and that this was the extent of the wedding.

There were no flowers. I want to be clear about that, because in eleven months of preparing for this day I had never once thought about flowers, and the omission did not occur to me until I was standing in a room with a wet window and a smell of floor polish and old radiators, and I thought: *no flowers*, and felt nothing about it at all. That was the moment I should have been afraid. Not of him. Of me.

Elliot signed first. He took the pen out of the little wooden tray and turned it once in his fingers to find the balance, and he signed in a hand so even it looked printed, and he did not hurry. Then he set the pen down parallel to the edge of the register, which is a thing I have since learned he does with everything: knives, letters, the remote control, his own wristwatch when he takes it off at night. He makes the world parallel before he will consent to be in it.

Then it was my turn.

I have spent nine years of my life repairing other people's paper. I know what a signature is. A signature is not a name; it is a pressure curve, a hesitation, the little involuntary hop where the nib lifts between letters. A signature is the most honest document a person ever makes, and it is also the easiest to forge. I can copy any hand I have studied for an hour. I have been paid to do it, once or twice, by people who wanted a letter from a dead mother to feel real.

I signed "Nell Ash" and the loop on the *l* came out exactly the way it always does, small and tight, like a stitch pulled too hard, and I hated it, and I signed it anyway.

The registrar said a thing about the nature of the contract. She used the word *contract*. Most people don't notice, and I noticed, and I looked at Elliot to see whether he had heard it too, and he had his eyes on the middle distance with the expression of a man listening to a piece of music he had chosen.

"Congratulations, Mrs. Crane," the woman said, and my whole body went cold, and the cold started in my left hand.

---

Here is what I knew about Elliot Crane on the day I married him.

I knew that he was thirty-eight years old and the sole principal of Crane & Voss Marine Assurance, which has occupied the fourth floor of the Assurance Building since 1881 and which holds the paper on one in every six hulls that comes into that harbor. I knew he was born in the house on the hill with the bad roof and that his mother had died when he was nineteen and his father when he was twenty-six. I knew he took his coffee black and standing, and that he had not been seen at the harbor club since the year I turned nineteen. I knew the make of his car, the name of his tailor, the fact that he kept the lifeboat funded and the fact that he had never married.

And I knew, because I am a conservator and I can read a document the way a doctor reads a chest, that the survey certificate he signed on the fourth of October in the year I was fifteen was a lie.

It was framed. That was the part I could never get past. In his own study, hung where a man hangs a degree, he had framed the certificate that passed the *Belladonna* as seaworthy eleven days before she broke her back on Gannet Rock and took forty-one men down with her — and he had not put it in a drawer. He had put it where he could see it. He had put it where anyone could see it.

I had been in this marriage for four hours and I had already decided what that meant, and I was wrong about it, but that comes later.

---

The man with the hat held the door. Elliot gave him a note, folded once, and did not look at him while he did it. Outside, the fog was so thick that the harbor had been erased, and the streetlights were only suggestions, and the whole city sounded like a room with a towel stuffed under the door.

"Have you eaten?" he said.

"Not since this morning."

"Then we'll eat on the boat."

He had said *on* the boat, not *before*. I filed that. He walked on the outside of the pavement, which is a courtesy so old-fashioned it has become a kind of statement, and when we came to the corner where the water first shows between the buildings, he put his hand out — not to take mine, but flat, at my elbow, the way you would shepherd someone across a road you believed to be more dangerous than they did.

I didn't take his arm. He didn't lower his hand for a full three seconds, and then he did.

"You'll want to change your shoes," he said.

These were the best shoes I owned.

"I'll be fine."

"The deck is wet."

"Then the deck is wet," I said, and something happened in his face that I had no name for then and have a name for now. It was the look of a man revising a figure upward.

---

### II

The taxi smelled of someone else's cigarettes and the heater made a noise like a kettle being reasoned with. Elliot sat with his hands on his knees, which is not how anyone sits in a taxi, and looked out at the fog, and said nothing for a mile and a half.

There is a thing that happens when you take a photograph of a person and then meet them. For eleven months I had been building Elliot Crane out of public record: the *Harbor Register*, the *Assurance Gazette*, the annual report of the lifeboat trust, the port authority's minutes, a photograph of him at a haulage inquiry in which he was the only man in the room not holding a pen. I had a file. It was not a romantic file. It was the kind of file a person makes when they intend to do something irrevocable and want to be able to say afterward that they had done their homework.

And none of it had prepared me for the fact that in a taxi he would sit forward, and breathe at a rate that was almost exactly even, and be quiet in a way that was not comfortable but was not hostile either. Quiet like a room with the window shut.

"You've changed your hair," he said.

I had. Two weeks ago, in a hotel bathroom, with a pair of nail scissors, at two in the morning. I had been drinking, a little. I don't drink, as a rule; I drink twice a year, alone, in hotels, and lose hours; that is a separate subject.

"It was getting long."

"It was," he agreed, and then, after a moment, "I preferred it."

The taxi went over the level crossing and the whole car made a noise.

"You'll find," Elliot said, "that I'm not good at this."

"At what?"

He considered the question with the seriousness of a man being sworn.

"Company," he said at last.

"Then it's convenient we're not going to have any."

I said it lightly. It was the first thing I had said all day that was entirely true, and it landed between us on the seat like a dropped key, and he looked at me for the length of a breath too long and then — this is the thing I keep coming back to, this is the thing I would testify to — he smiled. Not a warm smile. A small, quiet, satisfied one, the way a man might smile at a chess move he had not seen coming and was glad of.

"Quite right," he said.

---

The harbor at Craye is old and it works, which is not the same thing as being alive. There are two hundred yards of granite quay that the town has been trying to repair since the eighties; there is a fish market that smells of the fish market; there are four cranes standing over a container yard that has been at a quarter capacity for as long as I have been an adult. And there is the water, which is the wrong grey, the grey of a wet slate roof, and which in the fog had no horizon at all and simply became air at some point that you could not identify.

The boat was not a launch. I had expected a launch. It was a broad, high-sided workboat with a wheelhouse amidships and a deck cluttered with galvanized boxes and a coil of rope that had been deliberately, beautifully stowed.

"*Gannet*," I said, reading the transom.

"She's named for the rock," he said. "It's not a tribute."

"No."

"Get in the middle," he said. "Not the bow. The bow slams."

I got in the middle. I sat on a crate that had a blanket folded on it — not thrown, folded, with the corners tucked — and he cast off and turned the boat with a competence that was somehow restful, and the quay went away into the fog, and then the harbor, and then the last of the light.

And then it was just the engine, and the water, and a man I had known for eleven months and been married to for four hours, standing at a wheel with his coat buttoned to the throat.

I put my hand flat on the crate beside me and felt the tideline of every boat I have ever been on.

Here is a thing about me that I have never told anyone: I cannot swim.

---

### III

It takes forty minutes to get out to Marrow Point in good weather.

I know that now the way I know the way to my own front door. In the fog, on that first afternoon, it took an hour and ten, because he slowed twice for traffic that wasn't there and once, at the Bell, for nothing at all that I could see or hear, and then ran on.

"Are we stopping?" I said.

"No."

"You slowed."

"The water's shallow on this line at the ebb. I like to hear it."

He said it as though hearing the water were a normal thing to like, and I sat in the middle of his boat and thought: *he knows the sound of the ground under him.* And I thought: *he has been out here a very long time.*

He had brought food, because he had said we would eat on the boat, and he had meant it. It was in a flat tin box with a hasp, and it was: two rounds of bread and butter with the crusts cut off, a wedge of hard cheese, four dates, a thermos of tea, and a small screw-top jar of salt. He handed it to me and went back to the wheel.

I ate because it was there. The bread was not fresh. He had made it that morning anyway.

"You're not eating," I said.

"I'll eat when we're in."

"That's not a reason. That's a rule."

There was a silence of about four seconds, filled by the engine.

"Yes," he said.

There it was again. That was the thing I had not had in the file. A man who does not defend himself.

I thought — and I want this on the record, because it was the exact turn of the whole machine, and I want to be able to say I saw it — I thought: *this is going to be much harder than I planned.*

And then I thought: *no. It isn't. This is the easiest mark in the world. He wants to be understood.*

I have thought about that second thought a great deal since.

---

The tea was hot and had been made with the bag left in, and I drank it with my gloves on, which is hard, and which he noticed, and which he said nothing about, and which he was, I understood, filing.

I have a way of testing people. I do it in the conservation lab and I did it that afternoon without deciding to. You put something small and true in front of a person and you watch what their hands do.

So I said, "There are nine windows on that house."

We couldn't see the house. We were in the middle of a fog bank with the engine in it.

"How do you know that?" he said.

"The *Harbor Register* ran a piece on it when it came up for sale. Nine windows, all sea-facing, no shelter on the north elevation. The article was very rude about the north elevation."

"It is very rude," he said. "The wind comes through the north elevation like a disappointed relative."

I laughed. Actually laughed, out there in the fog, and I was annoyed with myself for it, and he heard the annoyance, I think, because he glanced over.

Then his hands went still on the wheel. Not a flinch. A stillness. You would not have seen it at all unless you had been watching his hands for an hour and had begun to understand that they are never still, that they are always doing something — turning a pen, straightening a pen, testing a pen.

"Ask me the thing you actually want to know," he said.

The engine turned over. The fog went past.

"All right," I said. "Why the north elevation? Anywhere else on that point you could have built. The whole lee of the hill is sheltered. You built into the worst corner of it and you put nine windows facing the water."

"I didn't build it. A keeper built it in 1881."

"You bought it."

"I did."

"Why?"

There was a longer pause. The tea was going cold in the cup and I did not drink it, because I did not want to be doing anything.

"Because," he said, "you can see the rock from every room."

He said it plainly. He said it the way another man would say *because it has a garden*.

I said, "There's nothing on the rock."

"There's a great deal on the rock," Elliot Crane said, and put his hand back on the wheel.

---

### IV

The fog lifted at four o'clock the way fog does, all at once and from the top down, and Marrow Point came out of it from the crown first: the signal station, squat and white and blind with its empty lamp-room, and then the house below it, built into the lee of the granite the way a hand fits into a glove, with its slate roof and its nine windows and a wall of black stone running out along the headland to a smear of white where the swell was breaking over something that was not quite a rock and was not quite anything.

"That's the Gannet," he said.

The swell went over it, and the white of it was very bright against that grey, and then the water drew back and there was a black shape in the gap, low and long and broken-backed, and then the swell covered it again.

I did not move. I want to be able to say that I did not move.

"That's her," I said. It was not a question.

"That's the wreck of the *Belladonna*," he said, with terrible care, giving each word its full weight, as though there were other wrecks and he wished to be precise. "She lies in nine fathoms at the stern and four at the bow. She's visible at low water on a spring tide. Which is today. Which is why we came today."

"Why did we come today?"

"Because you'd have found the path down to the water on your own in a week, and I'd rather be with you the first time you saw her," he said.

The engine was in neutral. The boat was moving on its own momentum and the tide, and the cold was coming up off the rock in a way that made my eyes water, and I sat on a galvanized box with a blanket folded on it and did arithmetic.

*He timed the wedding to the tide.*

Nobody times a wedding to the tide. A man who times a wedding to the tide has a reason, and the reason is not sentiment.

"Mr. Crane—"

"Elliot."

"Elliot." The word was dry in my mouth, like a pill. "Why did you marry me?"

He looked at me for a long moment. He was a very good-looking man in a way that had nothing to do with features; it was the stillness, and the coat, and the fact that his attention, when you had it, was total and unshared and made you feel briefly like the only object in a room with a great many objects in it.

"Because," he said, "I've been alone in that house for eleven years, and I have run out of ways to be alone in it."

It was a beautiful answer. It was the best answer he had, and it was not true, and I knew it was not true, and — this is the part I have never been able to explain to anyone, including a lawyer, including myself — I wanted it to be true so badly that my eyes stung, and I let that happen, and I let him see it happen, and I turned my face into the wind so that he would think it was the cold.

I have spent my whole life being the person in the room who notices. It is not a gift. It is what you become when you are fifteen and in a locker and everything you do not notice kills you. And that afternoon, on that boat, for the first time, I noticed something and chose not to act on it.

That is not a decision. That is a diagnosis.

----

---

The path up from the landing is cut into the rock in steps, one hundred and forty of them, and they are wet all the time, and there is a rope on iron rings that has been there so long the rope has gone the colour of the rock. He carried my case and did not offer to carry me, which I noticed and appreciated, in that order.

Halfway up, I stopped. My calves were burning and my left hand had begun to ache in the way it does in the cold — old burn, old nerve damage, it does not like October — and I put my palm flat against the stone and looked back down.

The boat was a toy. The quay at Craye was a grey nothing. And the water went out and out and there was no land in any direction except the land I had just come from, which was now a headland, which was now a place you would have to be taken to.

"Is it always this quiet?" I said.

"Always."

"No neighbours."

"No neighbours."

"No staff."

"Mrs. Quist comes Tuesdays and Fridays. She has been here thirty years. She was my father's."

"So on Wednesdays there are two of us on this point."

"Yes."

"And on Mondays."

"One," he said, "but not for much longer."

I climbed the rest of the steps because my legs were going to shake in a moment and I did not want him to see them shake.

---

The house smelled of cold stone and beeswax and something faintly, unpleasantly sweet that I could not place until I got to the hall, where I found it: a small brass bell, hanging from a bracket on the wall, and in the bowl of the bell an inch of water with a drowned moth in it and a residue of oil, and the oil was what I could smell. Somebody kept this bell oiled. Somebody kept it polished and oiled and dried, and somebody had forgotten it this week, and the rain had got in.

The hall was bigger than it should have been for a keeper's house, and the reason was obvious: at some point in the twentieth century somebody had taken out a wall. There was a radiator with a towel over it. There was a hat stand with nothing on it. There was a mirror, and the mirror was turned to the wall.

I stopped in front of the mirror and looked at the back of it — the old, thick, foxed glass, the frame with its pattern of rope and anchors — and then I looked down the hall.

There was a door at the end of it, and the door was open, and light was coming out of it, and there were other lights in the hall but the light in that room was yellow and steady and it was a lamp, not a ceiling bulb, and the room was the only warm room in the house.

I could see, from where I was standing, half of the far wall.

There was a nail in it. And beneath the nail, a fresh clean rectangle in the paint where something had been hanging and was not hanging now. And to the left of that clean rectangle there were rows of small brass plates, screwed to the wall at eye height in five uneven lines, and each plate had a name cut into it and a date, and the brass was polished to the point where it was almost white.

I counted them because I always count them.

Five, ten, twelve in the top row. Nine. Eight. Seven. Five.

Forty-one.

And one bare nail.

---

"Mrs. Crane."

He was behind me. He had my case in one hand and he had not put it down.

"That room's not finished," he said.

"Who are they?"

"Men."

"I can see they're men." My voice came out wrong. Flat and high. "Who are they, Elliot?"

He set the case down on the flags. He did it slowly, and he straightened up, and he looked past me at the open door with an expression I have seen on exactly one other kind of face in my life: the face of a man at a graveside who has not decided yet whether he is going to be able to do this.

"They were the crew of the *Belladonna*," he said.

The house was so quiet that I could hear the water in the bell.

"You keep them on your wall."

"I keep them in my house," he said.

"Why?"

And he said — and this is the answer that has cost me more than any other answer in my life, this is the answer I have thought about at three in the morning for a year, this is the sentence that I would give anything to be able to unhear —

"Because nobody else does."

Then he walked past me and closed the door, and it did not quite fit its frame, and he had to put his shoulder to it, and it went with a sound like a held breath.

---

Later — much later, when the fog had thinned and the tide had turned and I was in a strange bed in a strange house with my shoes on because I have never in my life been able to take my shoes off in a room I could not leave — I lay in the dark and counted.

Forty-one plates. Forty-one names. Forty-one men in the water at 02:40 on the third of October, twelve years ago, while a fifteen-year-old girl lay in a chain locker with her hands over her ears and a lantern burning a hole in her left palm.

Forty-one.

I have never once, in twelve years, been able to say the next number.

I said it into the dark of my husband's house, very quietly, the way you say a word in a language you are not sure you are allowed to speak.

"Forty-two."

And in the corridor, on the other side of my door, someone stopped walking.

And stood there.

And did not knock.

---

*End of Chapter One*
